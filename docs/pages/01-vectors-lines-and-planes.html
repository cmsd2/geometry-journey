1.01 — Vectors, Lines and Planes¶

Geometry begins with two operations on vectors in $\mathbb R^3$, and almost everything in this stage is downstream of them.

The dot product $a \cdot b = |a||b|\cos\theta$ knows about length and angle. It is the algebraic form of the Euclidean metric, and it is exactly the structure that the isometries of Stage 1.02–1.03 are defined to preserve.

The cross product $a \times b$ knows about area and orientation. It produces a vector perpendicular to both, of length equal to the area of the parallelogram they span, pointing in the direction fixed by the right-hand rule.

This notebook develops both, then uses them to answer the basic metric questions of solid geometry:

  1. Angles, projections, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
  2. Areas and volumes via the cross and scalar triple products.
  3. Distance from a point to a line, and from a point to a plane.
  4. Distance between two skew lines — the one that genuinely needs all three products at once.

Throughout, the aim is to derive each distance formula rather than quote it, and to check every derivation symbolically.

1. The dot product: length and angle¶

For $a, b \in \mathbb R^n$,

$$a \cdot b \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i \;=\; |a|\,|b|\cos\theta .$$

The first expression is a definition; the second is a theorem (it follows from the law of cosines applied to the triangle with sides $a$, $b$, $a-b$). Taking the two together is what lets algebra answer geometric questions.

Two consequences used constantly:

  • Orthogonality. $a \perp b \iff a \cdot b = 0$. Note this makes the zero vector orthogonal to everything.
  • Projection. The component of $b$ along $a$ is $$\operatorname{proj}_a b \;=\; \frac{a \cdot b}{a \cdot a}\, a ,$$ and $b - \operatorname{proj}_a b$ is orthogonal to $a$. This single decomposition is the seed of Gram–Schmidt, of least squares, and of the orthogonal projections that reappear in every later stage.

Cauchy–Schwarz, from the projection¶

The projection identity proves the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in one line. Write $b = \operatorname{proj}_a b + r$ with $r \perp a$. Then by Pythagoras,

$$|b|^2 \;=\; \frac{(a\cdot b)^2}{|a|^2} + |r|^2 \;\ge\; \frac{(a\cdot b)^2}{|a|^2},$$

so $(a \cdot b)^2 \le |a|^2 |b|^2$, with equality exactly when $r = 0$, i.e. when $b$ is a multiple of $a$.

This is why $\cos\theta = (a\cdot b)/(|a||b|)$ is a legitimate definition of angle: the inequality is precisely the statement that the right-hand side lies in $[-1, 1]$. Let us verify the identity symbolically in the plane, where the algebra is small enough to read.

v : [v1, v2]$

2. The cross product: area and orientation¶

That last output is worth pausing on. The Cauchy–Schwarz gap factored as $(u_1v_2 - u_2v_1)^2$ — a perfect square, and one we recognise as $\det\begin{pmatrix}u_1&u_2\\v_1&v_2\end{pmatrix}^2$. The "slack" in Cauchy–Schwarz is the squared area of the parallelogram spanned by $u$ and $v$. Zero slack means zero area means parallel vectors.

In $\mathbb R^3$ the same quantity becomes a vector. Define

$$a \times b \;=\; \det\begin{pmatrix} e_1 & e_2 & e_3 \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{pmatrix} \;=\; (a_2b_3 - a_3b_2,\; a_3b_1 - a_1b_3,\; a_1b_2 - a_2b_1).$$

Its defining properties:
Orthogonality $a \times b \perp a$ and $a \times b \perp b$
Magnitude $\lvert a \times b\rvert = \lvert a\rvert\,\lvert b\rvert \sin\theta$ — the area of the parallelogram
Antisymmetry $a \times b = -\,b \times a$, so $a \times a = 0$
Orientation direction given by the right-hand rule

The identity tying it to the dot product is Lagrange's identity, the $\mathbb R^3$ version of what we just factored:

$$\lvert a \times b\rvert^2 + (a \cdot b)^2 = \lvert a\rvert^2 \lvert b\rvert^2 .$$

A warning worth absorbing early: the cross product is a peculiarity of three dimensions. What generalises is the wedge product $a \wedge b$, an element of $\Lambda^2\mathbb R^n$ with $\binom n2$ components — which happens to be $3$ when $n = 3$, letting us disguise a bivector as a vector. Stage 4 onwards uses the wedge; this coincidence does not survive.

3. The scalar triple product: volume and coplanarity¶

Combining both products gives

$$[a, b, c] \;=\; a \cdot (b \times c) \;=\; \det\begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3\end{pmatrix},$$

the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the three vectors. The sign records orientation: positive for a right-handed triple, negative for left-handed.

Because it is a determinant, it inherits two properties for free:

  • Cyclic invariance. $[a,b,c] = [b,c,a] = [c,a,b]$, while swapping any two entries flips the sign.
  • Degeneracy test. $[a,b,c] = 0$ exactly when the three vectors are linearly dependent — that is, coplanar. A vanishing volume is the geometric face of a vanishing determinant.

This is the first appearance of a theme that runs the whole journey: a determinant is a volume, and the sign of a determinant is an orientation.

4. Lines, and the distance from a point to a line¶

A line through $p_0$ with direction $d \ne 0$ is the set

$$\ell = \{\, p_0 + t\,d \;:\; t \in \mathbb R \,\}.$$

To find the distance from a point $x$ to $\ell$, set $w = x - p_0$ and split $w$ into the part along $d$ and the part perpendicular to it. The perpendicular part is what survives:

$$\operatorname{dist}(x, \ell) \;=\; \bigl|\, w - \operatorname{proj}_d w \,\bigr| \;=\; \frac{\lvert w \times d\rvert}{\lvert d\rvert}.$$

The second form follows from $\lvert w \times d\rvert = \lvert w\rvert\,\lvert d\rvert\sin\theta$, since $\lvert w\rvert \sin\theta$ is the perpendicular height.

Rather than take that on trust, we can minimise $f(t) = \lvert p_0 + td - x\rvert^2$ directly and confirm the two agree. This is the honest version of the derivation: the distance is defined as a minimum, so we should compute the minimum.

In [38]:

/* t* is exactly the projection coefficient (w·d)/(d·d) */

/* The minimum value equals the cross-product formula.  Both expand to a
   large polynomial, so we compare them rather than display them. */
dmin2  : ratsimp( subst(t = tstar, f) )$

5. Planes, and the distance from a point to a plane¶

A plane is most efficiently described not by what it contains but by what it is perpendicular to. Given a normal $n \ne 0$ and a point $p_0$ on it,

$$\Pi = \{\, x \;:\; n \cdot (x - p_0) = 0 \,\} \;=\; \{\, x \;:\; n \cdot x = c \,\}, \qquad c = n \cdot p_0 .$$

Expanding gives the familiar $Ax + By + Cz = D$, and we can now read off what the coefficients mean: $(A, B, C)$ is a normal vector. That is not a mnemonic, it is the definition.

Distance is again a projection — this time we keep the component along $n$ rather than perpendicular to it:

$$\operatorname{dist}(x, \Pi) \;=\; \frac{\lvert n \cdot (x - p_0)\rvert}{\lvert n\rvert} \;=\; \frac{\lvert n\cdot x - c\rvert}{\lvert n\rvert}.$$

Note the pleasing duality with the line case. For a line we took the perpendicular component and needed a cross product; for a plane we take the parallel component and need only a dot product. A plane in $\mathbb R^3$ is a codimension-one object, and codimension one is exactly the situation a single linear equation describes.

Three points determine a plane. Given non-collinear $p, q, r$, the vectors $q-p$ and $r-p$ span it, so $n = (q-p) \times (r-p)$ is normal to it.

6. Skew lines — where all three products meet¶

Two lines in $\mathbb R^3$ that neither intersect nor run parallel are called skew. This cannot happen in the plane, and it is the first genuinely three-dimensional phenomenon we meet.

Let $\ell_1 = p_1 + s\,d_1$ and $\ell_2 = p_2 + t\,d_2$. The vector $n = d_1 \times d_2$ is perpendicular to both directions, so it points along the unique common perpendicular. Projecting the gap $p_2 - p_1$ onto that direction gives

$$\operatorname{dist}(\ell_1, \ell_2) \;=\; \frac{\lvert (p_2 - p_1) \cdot (d_1 \times d_2)\rvert}{\lvert d_1 \times d_2\rvert} \;=\; \frac{\bigl\lvert\,[\,p_2 - p_1,\; d_1,\; d_2\,]\,\bigr\rvert}{\lvert d_1 \times d_2\rvert}.$$

Every tool from this notebook appears at once: a triple product on top, a cross product underneath, and a projection as the idea.

The numerator has an independent meaning. It is the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by $p_2-p_1$, $d_1$ and $d_2$ — so the formula reads distance = volume / base area, which is the familiar relation for a prism read backwards. And it makes the degenerate case transparent: the volume vanishes exactly when the three vectors are coplanar, which is exactly when the lines meet or are parallel.

Below we minimise $\lvert(p_1 + s d_1) - (p_2 + t d_2)\rvert^2$ over both parameters and check that the minimum agrees.

Seeing the minimum¶

The distance between two skew lines is the minimum of a function of two variables, and that function is a positive-definite quadratic — a paraboloid. Plotting $h(s,t)$ makes the structure visible: a single bowl with one stationary point, which is why the two-line problem always has a unique answer (given non-parallel directions).

The floor of the bowl sits at height $\operatorname{dist}^2 = 1024/75 \approx 13.65$, never at zero — the lines never touch.

Summary¶

Quantity Formula What it measures
$a \cdot b$ $\sum a_i b_i = \lvert a\rvert\lvert b\rvert\cos\theta$ length and angle
$\operatorname{proj}_a b$ $\dfrac{a\cdot b}{a \cdot a}\,a$ the component along $a$
$a \times b$ $(a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\;\ldots)$ area and orientation
$[a,b,c]$ $a \cdot (b \times c) = \det(a;b;c)$ signed volume
$\operatorname{dist}(x,\ell)$ $\lvert w \times d\rvert / \lvert d\rvert$ drop the parallel part
$\operatorname{dist}(x,\Pi)$ $\lvert n\cdot(x-p_0)\rvert / \lvert n\rvert$ keep the normal part
$\operatorname{dist}(\ell_1,\ell_2)$ $\lvert[p_2-p_1,d_1,d_2]\rvert / \lvert d_1\times d_2\rvert$ volume ÷ base area

Every distance formula here came from the same move: split a vector into a component along something and a component perpendicular to it, then keep whichever one the problem cares about. That is orthogonal decomposition, and it will not stop being the answer — it returns as Gram–Schmidt in linear algebra, as the normal component defining the second fundamental form in Stage 3.04, and as the splitting of a connection into horizontal and vertical parts in Stage 5.05.

Two things to carry forward¶

The determinant is a volume. We saw it twice: the Cauchy–Schwarz gap factored into a squared $2\times 2$ determinant, and the triple product was a $3\times 3$ determinant. When Stage 3 computes surface area with $\sqrt{EG-F^2}$ and Stage 4 writes the Riemannian volume element as $\sqrt{\det g}$, they are using this same fact.

The cross product is a three-dimensional accident. Orthogonality, area and orientation all generalise; packaging them into a vector does not. Stage 4 replaces $a \times b$ with the wedge product $a \wedge b$, and the familiar identities reappear in a form that works in every dimension.
