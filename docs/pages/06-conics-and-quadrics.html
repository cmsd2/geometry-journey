1.06 — Conics and Quadrics¶

A conic is the zero set of a general quadratic in two variables,

$$Q(x,y) = a_{11}x^2 + 2a_{12}xy + a_{22}y^2 + 2b_1 x + 2b_2 y + c = 0,$$

and a quadric is the same thing one dimension up. The classical treatment — completing squares, memorising discriminants — obscures what is really happening. In matrix form,

$$Q(x) = x^{\mathsf T}\!A\,x + 2b^{\mathsf T}x + c, \qquad A = A^{\mathsf T},$$

and the entire classification is a statement about the eigenvalues of $A$. Nothing else is needed.

The reason is the spectral theorem: a real symmetric matrix can be orthogonally diagonalised, $A = R^{\mathsf T}\Lambda R$ with $R$ a rotation. So after rotating to the eigenvector basis and translating to the centre, every conic takes the form

$$\lambda_1 u^2 + \lambda_2 v^2 = k,$$

and only the signs of $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, k$ matter. Since rotations and translations are exactly the isometries of 1.02, this is a classification up to $E(2)$ — the shapes are genuinely different, not merely differently placed.

$\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_1), \operatorname{sign}(\lambda_2)$ $\det A$ Curve
same signs $> 0$ ellipse (or empty, or a point)
opposite signs $< 0$ hyperbola (or two crossing lines)
one zero $= 0$ parabola (or parallel lines, or empty)

This notebook derives that table, computes principal axes explicitly, and then does the three-dimensional case where the same logic produces the ellipsoid, the two hyperboloids, and the saddle.

1. Matrix form, and the two things that classify a conic¶

Write the conic homogeneously, absorbing the linear and constant terms into one symmetric $3\times3$ matrix:

$$Q(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix}x & y & 1\end{pmatrix} M \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & b_1 \\ a_{12} & a_{22} & b_2 \\ b_1 & b_2 & c \end{pmatrix}.$$

The top-left $2\times2$ block $A$ carries the quadratic part; $M$ carries everything.

Two determinants do all the classifying:

  • $\det A$ decides the type — ellipse, hyperbola or parabola — because its sign is the product of the eigenvalues.
  • $\det M$ decides whether the conic is non-degenerate. When $\det M = 0$ the conic collapses into lines or a point.

Finding the centre. A centre is a point of symmetry, and shifting $x \to x + x_0$ changes the linear term to $2(Ax_0 + b)^{\mathsf T}x$. So the linear term disappears exactly when

$$A x_0 = -b .$$

If $A$ is invertible there is a unique centre — ellipses and hyperbolas are central conics. If $A$ is singular the equation usually has no solution at all, and that missing centre is precisely what a parabola is: a conic whose centre has run off to infinity. (Stage 2.04 makes that literal.)

Below we build the machinery and use it on a deliberately awkward rotated conic.

2. Principal axes: the spectral theorem doing geometry¶

The cross term $-4xy$ is not a feature of the ellipse; it is a feature of our coordinates. Removing it means finding the directions in which the quadratic form is "pure", and those are the eigenvectors of $A$.

The spectral theorem guarantees this always works for a real symmetric matrix: the eigenvalues are real, and eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal. That orthogonality is the whole point — it means the change of basis is a rotation, so the shape is genuinely unchanged rather than distorted.

Writing $A = R\Lambda R^{\mathsf T}$ with $R \in SO(2)$ and setting $(u,v)^{\mathsf T} = R^{\mathsf T}(x - x_0)$:

$$\lambda_1 u^2 + \lambda_2 v^2 = k, \qquad k = -\det M / \det A .$$

For an ellipse the semi-axes are $\sqrt{k/\lambda_1}$ and $\sqrt{k/\lambda_2}$, so the larger eigenvalue gives the shorter axis — a large $\lambda$ means the form grows quickly in that direction, so the curve reaches $k$ sooner.

This is the first place where eigenvalues stop being an algebraic device and start being geometry. It is worth flagging that the pattern recurs, in almost exactly this form, at every later stage:

Symmetric object Eigenvalues are Where
quadratic form $A$ principal axes of a conic here
Hessian of a function directions of maximal curvature of a graph 3.05
shape operator of a surface principal curvatures $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ 3.04
Ricci tensor principal directions of volume distortion 4.05

The second fundamental form of a surface is literally a quadratic form, diagonalised in exactly this way. Everything below is rehearsal for that.

/* A complete classifier, driven only by det A, det M and the eigenvalue signs */
classify_conic(Q) := block([P, A, b, c0, Mx, dA, dM, λ, k, s],
  P : conic_parts(Q),  [A, b, c0] : P,
  Mx : ext_matrix(P),
  dA : determinant(A),  dM : determinant(Mx),
  /* normalise so that the quadratic part is "positive-leaning" */
  s  : if mat_trace(A) < 0 or (mat_trace(A) = 0 and dA = 0) then -1 else 1,
  A : s*A,  Mx : s*Mx,  dM : s^3*dM,
  λ  : eigenvalues(A)[1],
  if dA > 0 then (
    k : -dM/dA,
    if dM = 0 then "a single point"
    elseif k > 0 then "ellipse"
    else "empty (no real points)" )
  elseif dA < 0 then (
    if dM = 0 then "two crossing lines" else "hyperbola" )
  else ( /* dA = 0: the quadratic part is degenerate */
    if dM # 0 then "parabola"
    elseif A = zeromatrix(2,2) then "not a conic (linear)"
    else "two parallel, coincident, or no lines" ) )$

3. Quadrics: the same argument, one dimension up¶

Nothing about the method used $n = 2$. For $Q(x) = x^{\mathsf T}Ax + 2b^{\mathsf T}x + c$ with $A$ a symmetric $3\times3$ matrix, the spectral theorem again gives $A = R\Lambda R^{\mathsf T}$ with $R \in SO(3)$, and the normal form is

$$\lambda_1 u^2 + \lambda_2 v^2 + \lambda_3 w^2 = k .$$

The classification is by the signature of $A$ — the counts $(p, q, z)$ of positive, negative and zero eigenvalues — together with the sign of $k$.

Signature of $A$ $k$ Surface
$(3,0,0)$ $>0$ ellipsoid
$(3,0,0)$ $<0$ empty
$(2,1,0)$ $>0$ hyperboloid of one sheet
$(2,1,0)$ $<0$ hyperboloid of two sheets
$(2,1,0)$ $=0$ elliptic cone
$(2,0,1)$ — elliptic paraboloid, or elliptic cylinder
$(1,1,1)$ — hyperbolic paraboloid (the saddle), or hyperbolic cylinder

Two remarks worth carrying forward.

One sheet or two, from a single sign. The hyperboloids of one and two sheets have the same matrix $A$ and differ only in the sign of $k$ — the same surface family passing through the cone at $k = 0$. Watching a surface change topology as a parameter crosses zero is a preview of Morse theory.

The saddle is the model of negative curvature. The hyperbolic paraboloid $z = x^2 - y^2$ curves up in one direction and down in the other. In 3.05 we compute its Gaussian curvature and find $K < 0$, and in 2.06 the hyperbolic plane turns out to be the surface with $K < 0$ everywhere and constant. The signature $(1,1,\cdot)$ — one positive, one negative eigenvalue — is exactly what "negatively curved" will mean.

/* Signature of a symmetric matrix: (#positive, #negative, #zero) eigenvalues */
signature(A) := block([ev, λ, m, p, n, z],
  ev : eigenvalues(A), λ : ev[1], m : ev[2],
  p : 0, n : 0, z : 0,
  for i : 1 thru length(λ) do
    if λ[i] > 0 then p : p + m[i]
    elseif λ[i] < 0 then n : n + m[i]
    else z : z + m[i],
  [p, n, z] )$

Summary¶

The whole classification, in one line: write the quadratic as a symmetric matrix and look at the eigenvalues.

Step Tool Result
remove the linear term solve $Ax_0 = -b$ find the centre (or discover there isn't one)
remove the cross term spectral theorem, $A = R\Lambda R^{\mathsf T}$ principal axes
classify signs of $\lambda_i$ and of $k$ the type
detect degeneracy $\det M = 0$ lines, points, or nothing

For conics, $\det A > 0$ gives an ellipse, $\det A < 0$ a hyperbola, $\det A = 0$ a parabola. For quadrics the signature $(p,q,z)$ does the same job, and the hyperboloids of one and two sheets are distinguished only by the sign of a constant, passing through a cone as it changes.

What this notebook was really about¶

Three ideas will be used constantly later, and this is where they first do work.

A symmetric matrix is a shape. The eigenvalues are axis lengths, and their signs are the geometry. Stage 3.04 defines the shape operator of a surface as a symmetric operator, and its eigenvalues are the principal curvatures $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ — the same diagonalisation, with the same meaning.

Signature is the invariant. Not the eigenvalues themselves — those change under a general change of coordinates — but the number of positive, negative and zero ones. Sylvester's law of inertia says that count is basis-independent. In Stage 6.01 the signature $(1,3)$ of a Lorentzian metric is what makes spacetime spacetime.

Degeneracy is where the interesting things happen. $\det A = 0$ was the parabola — the conic whose centre escaped to infinity. $k = 0$ was the cone — the surface where two hyperboloids meet and the topology changes. Vanishing determinants mark the boundaries between regimes, and are where later stages will look for singularities, conjugate points and horizons.
