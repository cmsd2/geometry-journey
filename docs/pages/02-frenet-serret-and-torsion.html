3.02 — Frenet–Serret and Torsion¶

Curvature told us how fast a curve turns, but not in which direction it turns next. A circle and a helix can have the same curvature; what separates them is that the helix keeps escaping the plane it is momentarily turning in.

The tool is a moving frame: an orthonormal basis carried along the curve, adapted to the curve rather than to the ambient axes. For a unit-speed curve with $\kappa > 0$:

$$T = \gamma' \quad(\text{tangent}), \qquad N = \frac{T'}{\kappa} \quad(\text{normal}), \qquad B = T \times N \quad(\text{binormal}).$$

These are orthonormal at every point — the Frenet frame — and the whole local theory of space curves consists of describing how the frame rotates as you move along.

Because the frame stays orthonormal, its rate of change is constrained: differentiating $T\cdot T = 1$, $T \cdot N = 0$ and so on forces the derivative matrix to be antisymmetric. That is exactly the structure met in 1.03, where $\dot R R^{\mathsf T}$ was antisymmetric because $R$ stayed orthogonal. In three dimensions an antisymmetric matrix has three independent entries — but one of them vanishes here for a structural reason, leaving just two:

$$\begin{pmatrix} T' \\ N' \\ B'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \kappa & 0 \\ -\kappa & 0 & \tau \\ 0 & -\tau & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} T \\ N \\ B\end{pmatrix}.$$

These are the Frenet–Serret equations, and the new quantity $\tau$ is the torsion — the rate at which the curve twists out of its osculating plane.

The pay-off is the fundamental theorem of curves: $\kappa$ and $\tau$, as functions of arc length, determine the curve completely up to a rigid motion. Two numbers per point are the entire local geometry — and this is the exact analogue of 1.07, where pairwise distances determined a configuration up to $E(n)$.

1. Why the matrix is antisymmetric, and why one entry vanishes¶

Write the frame as the rows of a matrix $F = (T; N; B)$. Orthonormality says $FF^{\mathsf T} = I$, and differentiating gives

$$F'F^{\mathsf T} + F(F')^{\mathsf T} = 0,$$

so $A = F'F^{\mathsf T}$ satisfies $A + A^{\mathsf T} = 0$: antisymmetric. This is the identical computation to 1.03 §"measuring the degeneracy", and for the identical reason — a frame that stays orthonormal can only rotate, and infinitesimal rotations are antisymmetric. Stage 5 will call $A$ an element of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$.

An antisymmetric $3\times3$ matrix has three free entries, so a priori

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a & b \\ -a & 0 & c \\ -b & -c & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Now identify them:

  • $a = T'\cdot N = \kappa$, by the definition of $N$.
  • $b = T'\cdot B = \kappa N \cdot B = 0$. This is the entry that vanishes, and it does so by construction: $T'$ points along $N$, so it has no $B$-component at all.
  • $c = N'\cdot B =: \tau$, the definition of torsion.

So the two-parameter form is forced. The vanishing of $b$ is not a coincidence but a consequence of choosing $N$ to be the direction $T$ is turning towards.

Interpretation of $\tau$. The plane spanned by $T$ and $N$ is the osculating plane — the plane the curve is instantaneously turning in, containing the osculating circle of 3.01. Its normal is $B$, and $B' = -\tau N$. So

$$\tau = 0 \iff B \text{ is constant} \iff \text{the curve is planar}.$$

Torsion measures failure to be flat. A curve with $\kappa > 0$ and $\tau \equiv 0$ lies in a fixed plane; nonzero $\tau$ is exactly what makes a curve genuinely three-dimensional.

General formula. For an arbitrary regular parametrisation,

$$\tau = \frac{(\gamma' \times \gamma'')\cdot \gamma'''}{|\gamma'\times\gamma''|^2},$$

with the scalar triple product of 1.01 in the numerator — a signed volume, so $\tau$, unlike $\kappa$, carries a sign. Right-handed helices have $\tau > 0$, left-handed ones $\tau < 0$: chirality again, and this time it is a local quantity.

2. The fundamental theorem of curves¶

Theorem. Given smooth functions $\kappa(s) > 0$ and $\tau(s)$ on an interval, there exists a unit-speed curve with those invariants, and it is unique up to a rigid motion of $\mathbb R^3$.

Both halves follow from the theory of ODEs rather than from geometry.

Existence. The Frenet–Serret equations are a linear ODE system for the nine components of $(T,N,B)$, with coefficients built from $\kappa$ and $\tau$. Linear systems with smooth coefficients have solutions; and because the coefficient matrix is antisymmetric, an initially orthonormal frame stays orthonormal — the antisymmetry is exactly the condition preserving $FF^{\mathsf T} = I$. Then $\gamma(s) = \int T$ recovers the curve.

Uniqueness. The solution is determined by its initial data: a starting point $\gamma(0)$ and a starting frame $(T,N,B)(0)$. But a point plus a right-handed orthonormal frame is precisely the data of an element of $SE(3)$ — and changing it applies a rigid motion. So the curve is unique up to $SE(3)$.

This mirrors 1.07 exactly. There, pairwise distances determined a point configuration up to $E(n)$; here, two functions determine a curve up to $SE(3)$. In both cases a complete set of invariants has been found, and the ambiguity left over is precisely the group.

The demonstration below takes constant $\kappa = \tau = \tfrac12$, integrates the Frenet system numerically from an initial frame, and checks that the reconstructed curve is the helix $(\cos s/\sqrt2, \sin s/\sqrt2, s/\sqrt2)$ — which indeed has $\kappa = \tau = 1/2$ by the formulas above with $a = b = 1$.

We integrate with a hand-written fourth-order Runge–Kutta rather than a library routine, so that nothing about the method is hidden.

  g  : [u[1],u[2],u[3]],   T_ : [u[4],u[5],u[6]],

Summary¶

$$\begin{pmatrix} T' \\ N' \\ B'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \kappa & 0 \\ -\kappa & 0 & \tau \\ 0 & -\tau & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} T \\ N \\ B\end{pmatrix}$$

| | | |---|---| | $\kappa$ | rate of turning; $> 0$ by convention | | $\tau$ | rate of twisting out of the osculating plane; signed | | $\tau = 0$ | $\iff$ the curve is planar | | $\kappa, \tau$ constant | $\iff$ the curve is a helix (or circle if $\tau = 0$) | | general $\tau$ | $\dfrac{(\gamma'\times\gamma'')\cdot\gamma'''}{|\gamma'\times\gamma''|^2}$ |

Verified: the helix has $\kappa = a/(a^2+b^2)$, $\tau = b/(a^2+b^2)$; its Frenet frame is orthonormal and right-handed; all three Frenet–Serret equations hold identically; $F'F^{\mathsf T}$ came out as exactly the predicted antisymmetric matrix with the structurally forced zero in position $(1,3)$; a left-handed helix has the same $\kappa$ and opposite $\tau$; planar curves have $\tau = 0$ whether or not their plane is tilted; and integrating the Frenet system from constant $\kappa = \tau = \tfrac12$ reproduced the helix to within $5\times10^{-12}$ over a full turn, with the frame remaining orthonormal to $10^{-14}$.

Two things to carry forward¶

Orthonormal frame ⟹ antisymmetric derivative. This has now appeared three times: as $\dot R R^{\mathsf T}$ in 1.03, as the constraint forcing $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, and here as the shape of the Frenet matrix. It is the reason the local theory of curves has exactly two invariants rather than three — the third slot is forced to zero by the choice of $N$. In Stage 5 this becomes the general statement that a connection on an orthonormal frame bundle takes values in $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, and the moving-frame method generalises into Cartan's structure equations.

A complete set of invariants, modulo a group. $(\kappa, \tau)$ determine a curve up to $SE(3)$, just as pairwise distances determined a configuration up to $E(n)$ in 1.07. This is the recurring shape of every classification in geometry: find quantities unchanged by the group, prove they are complete, and the leftover ambiguity is the group.

Surfaces will need more than two numbers — the analogous role is played by the first and second fundamental forms, and the "fundamental theorem of surfaces" requires compatibility conditions (the Gauss and Codazzi equations of 3.06) that have no counterpart for curves. Curves are easy precisely because there is nothing for the invariants to be inconsistent about.

A note on the $\kappa > 0$ hypothesis. The Frenet frame is undefined where $\kappa = 0$, since $N = T'/\kappa$ divides by zero — at an inflection point the curve is not turning and there is no preferred normal. Torsion is likewise undefined there. Curves with isolated zeros of $\kappa$ therefore need care, and this is the reason the fundamental theorem is stated for $\kappa > 0$.
