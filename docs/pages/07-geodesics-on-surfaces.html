3.07 — Geodesics on Surfaces¶

What does "straight" mean on a curved surface?

A straight line in the plane is a curve with zero acceleration. On a surface that is impossible: a curve confined to a sphere must accelerate, or it would leave the sphere. But 3.04 showed that acceleration splits into two parts,

$$\gamma'' = \underbrace{(\gamma'')^{\top}}_{\text{tangential}} + \underbrace{\kappa_n\, n}_{\text{normal}},$$

and only the normal part is forced on us by the constraint. So:

A geodesic is a curve whose acceleration is purely normal — it does not turn within the surface, only as much as the surface itself compels.

The tangential part has a name: the geodesic curvature $\kappa_g$, measuring turning as felt by the ant. Then

$$\kappa^2 = \kappa_g^2 + \kappa_n^2,$$

splitting the total curvature of a curve into the part it chooses ($\kappa_g$) and the part the surface imposes ($\kappa_n$). Geodesics are exactly the curves with $\kappa_g \equiv 0$.

The equations. Writing the curve as $(u^1(t), u^2(t))$ in coordinates, the condition becomes

$$\ddot u^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\dot u^i \dot u^j = 0, \qquad k = 1, 2.$$

Only the Christoffel symbols appear — and 3.06 established that those depend on $E, F, G$ alone. So:

Geodesics are intrinsic. The ant can find them without knowing the surface is curved in space.

Two characterisations coincide, and it is worth keeping both in mind:

  • straightest — zero geodesic curvature, the definition above;
  • shortest — locally length-minimising, from the calculus of variations.

They agree because the Euler–Lagrange equations for arc length reduce to the same system. Stage 4.03 proves this properly; here we take it and compute.

Clairaut's relation¶

On a surface of revolution the geodesic equations have a conserved quantity, and it is both useful and geometrically vivid.

Take $\sigma = (f(u)\cos v,\, f(u)\sin v,\, g(u))$, so $\mathrm{I} = \mathrm{diag}(E, f^2)$ with $E = f'^2 + g'^2$. The coordinate $v$ does not appear in the metric — the surface is rotationally symmetric — and the second geodesic equation becomes a conservation law:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(f^2\,\dot v\right) = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f^2\,\dot v = \text{const}.$$

Writing $\psi$ for the angle between the geodesic and the meridian, this is

$$\boxed{\;f\,\sin\psi = \text{const}\;}$$

Clairaut's relation (1733). In words: the further a geodesic strays from the axis, the more nearly it runs along a meridian.

Why the conservation law exists. Because $v$ is a cyclic coordinate — the metric does not depend on it. That is Noether's theorem in its simplest form: a symmetry of the metric (here, rotation about the axis) yields a conserved quantity along geodesics. In Stage 4 such a symmetry is called a Killing field, and in 6.02 exactly this argument produces the conserved energy and angular momentum of orbits around a black hole — which is how the perihelion precession of Mercury is computed.

What it predicts. On a surface of revolution a geodesic starting at radius $f_0$ with angle $\psi_0$ satisfies $f\sin\psi = f_0\sin\psi_0$. Since $\sin\psi \le 1$, the geodesic can never reach a radius smaller than $f_0\sin\psi_0$ — it is turned back by a barrier. On a torus this traps some geodesics in the outer region forever; on a hyperboloid it keeps them away from the throat.

Summary¶

$$\ddot u^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\dot u^i\dot u^j = 0$$

Surface Geodesics
plane $\ddot u = \ddot v = 0$ — straight lines
sphere great circles (meridians and the equator verified; other parallels fail)
cylinder helices, including lines and circles as limiting cases
surface of revolution governed by Clairaut: $f\sin\psi = \text{const}$
torus no closed form — integrated numerically

Verified: the parallel at colatitude $\pi/6$ has residual $-\sqrt3/4 \ne 0$, so it is not a geodesic, while meridians and the equator are; $f^2\dot v$ is exactly conserved, $d/dt(f^2\dot v) - f^2\cdot(\text{eq. 2}) = 0$; and along numerically integrated torus geodesics the Clairaut invariant drifted by $4\times10^{-13}$ over 4000 RK4 steps.

The barrier is real. A geodesic launched from the outer equator of the torus ($f_0 = 4$) at $\psi_0 = \pi/3$ has $f\sin\psi = 3.46$, so it can only visit radii $\ge 3.46$ — colatitudes $|u| \le 1.088$. The numerics confirm it: after 4000 steps it was still at $u = 0.033$, oscillating in its band. Launched at $\psi_0 = \pi/8$ the invariant is $1.53 < 2$, no barrier exists, and it had wound to $u = 32.6$ — more than five times round the tube.

Three things this sets up¶

Geodesics are intrinsic, because the equations contain only Christoffel symbols and 3.06 showed those come from the metric. The ant finds the same "straight lines" as an observer in space. This is what makes Stage 4 possible: geodesics can be defined on a manifold with no ambient space at all.

Symmetry gives conservation. Clairaut's relation came from $v$ being absent from the metric. Stage 4 calls this a Killing field, and 6.02 uses precisely this mechanism — the Schwarzschild metric's independence of $t$ and $\phi$ — to extract the conserved energy and angular momentum that determine planetary orbits and light bending.

Geodesic curvature is what Gauss–Bonnet integrates. We defined $\kappa_g$ as the tangential part of acceleration and geodesics as $\kappa_g \equiv 0$. In 3.08 the boundary term of Gauss–Bonnet is $\oint\kappa_g\,ds$, which vanishes exactly when the boundary is made of geodesics — which is why the theorem takes its cleanest form on geodesic triangles, and why it reproduces the angle-deficit results of 2.06 and 2.07.

On the exponential map. Given $p$ and a tangent vector $X$, the geodesic starting at $p$ with velocity $X$ defines $\exp_p(X) = \gamma(1)$. Using it as a coordinate chart gives normal coordinates, in which $g_{ij}(p) = \delta_{ij}$ and $\Gamma^k_{ij}(p) = 0$ — the metric is flat to first order at $p$, and curvature appears only at second order. That construction is developed in 4.03; in relativity it is the mathematical content of the equivalence principle.
