2.05 — Inversive Geometry and Möbius Maps¶

This notebook takes the complex route into non-Euclidean geometry. The setting is the Riemann sphere

$$\hat{\mathbb C} = \mathbb C \cup \{\infty\},$$

the complex plane with a single point at infinity adjoined — note one point, not a whole line as in $\mathbb{RP}^2$. That difference matters and we will come back to it.

The group is the Möbius group

$$z \longmapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d}, \qquad ad - bc \ne 0,$$

which is $PGL_2(\mathbb C)$, and over $\mathbb C$ can be normalised to $ad-bc=1$, giving $PSL_2(\mathbb C)$.

Three facts make this geometry worth its own notebook:

Circles and lines stop being different things. A Möbius map sends circles to circles, provided we agree that a line is a circle through $\infty$. On the sphere this is literally true: under stereographic projection, lines in the plane become circles through the north pole. The distinction between "straight" and "curved" was an artefact of choosing where to stand.

Angles are preserved. Möbius maps are conformal. So although this group destroys distance — as any group containing $z \mapsto 2z$ must — it keeps angle. That is a different trade from the projective one, and it is what makes this geometry useful for analysis and for maps of the Earth.

Inversion generates it. Just as reflections generated the isometry group in 1.02, inversions in circles generate the Möbius group together with its orientation-reversing partners. The analogy with 1.02 is exact, and 1.02's three-reflection theorem has a counterpart here.

The cross-ratio returns as the central invariant, and by the end we will have the tools to define hyperbolic distance in 2.06.

1. Möbius maps and the four generators¶

Every Möbius map factors into elementary pieces. Provided $c \ne 0$,

$$\frac{az+b}{cz+d} = \frac{a}{c} + \frac{bc-ad}{c^2}\cdot\frac{1}{z + d/c},$$

which reads as: translate, invert, scale-and-rotate, translate. So the group is generated by

Generator Effect
$z \mapsto z + b$ translation
$z \mapsto \lambda z$ ($\lambda \in \mathbb C^*$) rotation and scaling
$z \mapsto 1/z$ inversion composed with a reflection

The first two are affine and unremarkable. Everything genuinely new comes from $z \mapsto 1/z$.

Matrix form. Composition of Möbius maps is matrix multiplication:

$$z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d} \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix},$$

with the matrix determined up to scale — hence $PGL_2(\mathbb C)$. This is the same identification we used in 2.03 for projectivities of a line, now over $\mathbb C$, and it means a Möbius map is a projectivity of $\mathbb{CP}^1$. The Riemann sphere and the complex projective line are the same object:

$$\hat{\mathbb C} \;=\; \mathbb{CP}^1 .$$

That explains the "one point at infinity": $\mathbb{CP}^1$ has one point outside the affine chart, exactly as $\mathbb{RP}^1$ does. It is $\mathbb{RP}^2$ that has a whole line at infinity, because it is 2-dimensional over $\mathbb R$ in a different way.

Three points determine the map. By 2.03's counting, $PGL_2$ has 3 complex parameters, so there is a unique Möbius map sending any three distinct points to any other three. The standard choice sends $(z_1, z_2, z_3) \mapsto (0, 1, \infty)$, and the map that does it is — unsurprisingly — the cross-ratio.

2. Circles go to circles¶

Write the general circle-or-line in the plane as

$$\alpha(x^2 + y^2) + Bx + Cy + \gamma = 0,$$

with $\alpha, B, C, \gamma$ real. When $\alpha \ne 0$ this is a circle; when $\alpha = 0$ it degenerates to a line. Calling both "circles" is the move that makes the theory uniform, and the sphere justifies it.

Since translations and scalings obviously preserve this family, only $z \mapsto 1/z$ needs checking. In complex form,

$$\frac1z = \frac{\bar z}{|z|^2}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad (x,y) \longmapsto \left(\frac{x}{x^2+y^2},\; \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\right).$$

Substituting and clearing denominators should return an equation of exactly the same shape — with $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ exchanged. That swap is the entire content of the theorem, and it says precisely:

Before $\alpha$ $\gamma$ After
circle not through $0$ $\ne 0$ $\ne 0$ circle not through $0$
circle through $0$ ($\gamma = 0$) $\ne 0$ $0$ line not through $0$
line not through $0$ ($\alpha = 0$) $0$ $\ne 0$ circle through $0$
line through $0$ $0$ $0$ line through $0$

So "circle" and "line" are not preserved separately — only their union is. A line is a circle that happens to pass through $\infty$, and inversion moves $\infty$ to $0$, which is why circles through the origin are exactly the ones that straighten out.

The concyclicity test¶

The complex cross-ratio gives a criterion that would be awkward to state any other way:

Four points of $\hat{\mathbb C}$ lie on a common circle or line if and only if their cross-ratio is real.

The proof is a two-liner given what we have. Send three of the points to $0, 1, \infty$ by a Möbius map — those three lie on the real line, together with $\infty$. The fourth point goes to $\lambda$, the cross-ratio. The image four points are concyclic exactly when $\lambda$ also lies on $\mathbb R \cup \{\infty\}$, i.e. when $\lambda$ is real. And since Möbius maps preserve both cross-ratio and the family of circles, the same holds before the map.

Read the other way round, this is the circles-to-circles theorem, obtained from cross-ratio invariance instead of by substitution — a second proof, and a shorter one.

It also explains what the real line is doing in this picture. $\mathbb R \cup \{\infty\}$ is just one circle among all the others; there is nothing distinguished about it until we choose to distinguish it. Choosing it — and keeping only the Möbius maps that preserve it and its upper side — is exactly how 2.06 will build the hyperbolic plane.

3. Angles survive: conformality¶

Möbius maps destroy distance but preserve angle. The reason is the one fact that makes complex analysis geometric:

A map that is complex-differentiable with $f'(z_0) \ne 0$ acts, to first order, as multiplication by the complex number $f'(z_0)$ — and multiplication by a complex number is a rotation composed with a scaling.

Rotations and scalings both preserve angles, so $f$ does too. Concretely, if $f = u + iv$ then the real Jacobian is

$$J = \begin{pmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} p & -q \\ q & p\end{pmatrix}, \qquad f' = p + iq,$$

and that shape — the Cauchy–Riemann equations $u_x = v_y$, $u_y = -v_x$ — is exactly "similarity matrix". Compare 1.02, where $A^{\mathsf T}A = I$ characterised the isometries; here $J$ is a scalar multiple of a rotation, so it is a similarity, preserving angle but not length.

Note also that Möbius maps preserve orientation — $\det J = p^2 + q^2 > 0$ — so they are the conformal half of the picture. The orientation-reversing counterparts involve $\bar z$, and among them is genuine circle inversion, which is the subject of §4.

4. Inversion, and the analogy with reflection¶

Inversion in the circle of centre $a$ and radius $r$ is

$$\iota(z) = a + \frac{r^2}{\overline{z - a}} .$$

Its properties line up, one for one, with those of a reflection in 1.02:

Reflection in a line (1.02) Inversion in a circle
fixes the line pointwise fixes the circle pointwise
an involution, $F^2 = I$ an involution, $\iota^2 = \mathrm{id}$
swaps the two half-planes swaps inside and outside
orientation-reversing orientation-reversing
composing two gives a rotation or translation composing two gives a Möbius map
generates $E(2)$ generates the Möbius group (with reflections)

The last row is the point. In 1.02, reflections generated everything and the classification followed from counting them. Here the same holds: the orientation-preserving maps are the products of an even number of inversions, and those are exactly the Möbius maps. A reflection in a line is itself the degenerate case — inversion in a "circle" of infinite radius.

The defining property $|\iota(z) - a|\cdot|z - a| = r^2$ says inversion trades distance-from-centre for its reciprocal, which is why it turns the inside of the circle into the outside and sends the centre to $\infty$.

Inversion is where the geometry stops being about $\mathbb C$ as a field and starts being about $\hat{\mathbb C}$ as a sphere — and that is the last thing to check.

5. The sphere behind it all¶

Stereographic projection maps the unit sphere minus its north pole $N = (0,0,1)$ onto the plane, by drawing the line from $N$ through a point of the sphere and taking where it crosses $z = 0$:

$$(X,Y,Z) \longmapsto \frac{X + iY}{1 - Z}, \qquad\text{with inverse}\qquad z \longmapsto \left(\frac{2x}{1+|z|^2},\ \frac{2y}{1+|z|^2},\ \frac{|z|^2-1}{|z|^2+1}\right).$$

Sending $N \mapsto \infty$ makes it a bijection $S^2 \to \hat{\mathbb C}$, and this is what the Riemann sphere is.

A circle on the sphere is its intersection with a plane $aX + bY + cZ = d$. Substituting the inverse projection should turn that plane equation into the general circle-or-line equation of §2 — and the degenerate case $\alpha = 0$ should occur exactly when the plane passes through $N$, i.e. when $c = d$.

If that works out, then:

Lines in the plane are precisely the images of circles through the north pole.

Which retires the distinction between lines and circles completely. They were the same objects all along; "straight" merely meant "passing through the point we removed".

This also gives the cleanest way to see why $\hat{\mathbb C}$ has one point at infinity rather than a line of them: the sphere has one north pole. And it identifies the Möbius group with the rotations of the sphere in the sub-case $PSU(2)$ — connecting back to 1.03's $SU(2) \to SO(3)$ double cover, which is the same group appearing for the same reason.

Three results were established by computation rather than assertion:

Circles map to circles, verified by substituting $z \mapsto 1/z$ into the general equation and finding $(\alpha, B, C, \gamma) \mapsto (\gamma, B, -C, \alpha)$. The $\alpha \leftrightarrow \gamma$ swap is why circles through the origin become lines.

Four points are concyclic iff their cross-ratio is real — checked on symmetric and asymmetric points of the unit circle, on four collinear points, and on a non-concyclic quadruple. This gives a second, shorter proof of circles-to-circles.

Stereographic projection turns plane equations into circle equations, with $\alpha = c - d$ vanishing exactly when the plane contains the north pole. So a line in the plane is precisely a circle through $\infty$ — not a convention, a theorem.

The set-up for hyperbolic geometry¶

Everything here has been leading somewhere specific. We now have a group acting on a sphere, an invariant (the cross-ratio), and a family of circles with no distinguished member.

2.06 breaks that symmetry deliberately. Nominate one circle — the real line $\mathbb R \cup \{\infty\}$ — and keep only the Möbius maps that preserve it and the upper half-plane above it. Those maps have real coefficients, so the subgroup is

$$PSL_2(\mathbb R) \;\subset\; PSL_2(\mathbb C),$$

and it turns out to act on the upper half-plane exactly as an isometry group. Distance is recovered as a logarithm of a cross-ratio taken against the nominated circle.

This is the Cayley–Klein pattern promised at the end of 2.04, arrived at from the complex side instead of the projective one. The two routes meet, which is the real reason both were worth taking.
